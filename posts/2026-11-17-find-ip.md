Which address you need depends on your task. When you set up a wireless printer or troubleshoot a smart TV, the local address keeps your devices communicating across your home router. When you need to whitelist an office connection, verify a VPN tunnel, or exclude your own visits from a web analytics dashboard, only the public address matters. 

## Find Your Public IP in Seconds

Every device connected directly to the internet requires a unique identifier to send and receive data, but because individual laptops and phones usually sit behind a router, the wider internet interacts only with the public-facing address assigned by your Internet Service Provider. 

You can find the IP address for your current connection quickly by using the [Swetrix IP Address Lookup tool](https://swetrix.com/tools/ip-lookup). Loading the page sends a request from your network to the server, and the utility reads the origin address attached to that request, returning the exact string of numbers your browser broadcast. 

Modern internet connections use two different protocols, and a robust lookup tool detects both. The older IPv4 standard formats addresses as four sets of numbers separated by periods, while the newer IPv6 features longer strings of alphanumeric characters separated by colons to compensate for the exhausted global supply of IPv4 addresses. If your provider assigns both to your network, the tool displays whichever protocol your browser used to initiate the request. 

A lookup utility identifies the connection point rather than the physical machine on your desk. If ten computers share the same office network, they broadcast identical public IP addresses to the outside world. Restarting your modem or switching from Wi-Fi to a cellular data connection can result in a different public address, so the lookup result may change.

## Public IP vs. Local IP

Network traffic operates on two distinct layers: your internet provider manages the public layer, while your router manages the private layer inside your building. By acting as a translator between these spaces, the router ensures incoming data reaches the correct screen without exposing every device to the open internet.

| Characteristic | Public IP | Local IP |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Visibility** | Visible to external websites, web analytics, and external servers. | Hidden behind the router; invisible to the wider internet. |
| **Assignment** | Assigned by your Internet Service Provider to your connection, or shared through its gateway. | Handed out to individual devices by your local router. |
| **Uniqueness** | Routable across the public internet, though it may be shared by multiple connections through Carrier-Grade NAT. | Unique only within your specific home or office network. |
| **Format** | Can be any valid public IPv4 or IPv6 string. | Usually follows a predictable private format, such as `192.168.x.x` or `10.x.x.x`. |

This division relies on Network Address Translation. When your phone requests a web page, it sends the request to the router using its local address. The router strips away that identifier, replaces it with the public IP, and forwards the request to the website. Upon receiving the reply, the router checks its internal log and routes the data back to the specific local address that asked for it. 

RFC 1918 specifies three blocks for [reserved private IPv4 address space](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/rfc1918/), which can be used by many enterprises. As a result, separate enterprises can use the same private address, such as `192.168.0.1`, and RFC 1918 says routing information about private networks should not be propagated across inter-enterprise links.

Neither type of IP address is the same as a MAC address, a link-layer identifier associated with a network interface. Devices may use a randomized MAC address, so it isn't necessarily a permanent identifier tied to the hardware.

## Find Your Device’s Local IP

Your system preferences reveal the internal network address assigned by your router. This local identifier helps you configure a firewall rule, set up a network drive, or test an application running on your own machine, but it is not the address a website sees.

The operating system requests this local address through a process called DHCP, and your router leases it to the device for a set period. Because these numbers can change after a system restart or a long period of inactivity, configuring a static assignment within the router’s administration panel provides a permanent local address for a dedicated server or printer.

### Windows

Microsoft puts network details in Network & internet settings. To check your [network settings in Windows](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/experience/connectivity-networking/essential-network-settings-and-tasks-in-windows), select Start > Settings > Network & internet.

Choose Wi-Fi and click your connected network's name, or select Ethernet for a wired connection. Scroll down the page to find the properties section, where the IPv4 address field displays your current local assignment alongside the IPv6 address if your network supports the newer protocol. 

### Mac

Apple groups connectivity options within System Settings. To view your [TCP/IP details](https://support.apple.com/en-bw/guide/mac-help/mh14129/mac), choose Apple menu > System Settings, click Network in the sidebar, click a network service, click Details, and then click TCP/IP.

The TCP/IP pane displays the local IP address, the subnet mask, and the router address. Typing the router address into a browser usually loads the administrative login screen for your home network.

### iPhone

For the network you're connected to, Apple says you can change its Wi-Fi settings on iPhone, as described in its [Wi-Fi settings guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/manage-wi-fi-settings-iphw5gjwl8k2/27/ios/27).

Scroll down to the IPv4 Address section to see your local assignment, which sits near the IPv6 details if your carrier uses the updated standard. 

### Android

Android devices display this information in their Wi-Fi settings, though the menu path varies depending on the manufacturer. Tapping the gear icon next to your active connection in the internet settings generally reveals the local address, but checking specific manufacturer documentation offers the most reliable route.

## Exclude Your Own Visits From Swetrix Analytics

Accurate traffic data helps content creators, bloggers, and marketing agencies measure campaign success and audience growth. Editing a landing page, testing a new checkout flow, or reviewing published posts generates pageviews. If your analytics platform tracks those actions, conversion rates drop and engagement metrics reflect your own behavior rather than customer interest.

Maintaining clean data requires filtering internal traffic, and configuring these filters in Swetrix requires your public IP address. Because the analytics tracker runs on the external website, it only sees the public-facing address your router presents to the internet. Entering a local address like `10.0.0.5` into a filter accomplishes nothing, as the tracker never encounters that private identifier.

The [documentation for excluding your own visits](https://swetrix.com/docs/exclude-own-visits) outlines the process. After copying your public address from a lookup tool, open your Swetrix project settings and paste the numbers into the exclusion list. Depending on your internet provider, you may need to add both the IPv4 and IPv6 versions of your address to ensure comprehensive filtering.

Filtering by public IP introduces a few technical realities to plan around:

*   **Shared networks affect everyone:** If you work in a shared office and block the office connection, Swetrix ignores visits from all colleagues on that same network, as the public address represents the whole building.
*   **Dynamic addresses change over time:** Most residential internet providers assign dynamic IP addresses. If your modem reboots or your lease expires, the provider might assign a new address. Your analytics platform will then start tracking your visits again until you update the exclusion list with the new numbers.
*   **Remote work requires multiple rules:** If you manage a site from home, a coffee shop, and a co-working space, you need to find and exclude the public IP for each location you visit frequently.

When managing dynamic networks becomes too cumbersome, or when you only want to exclude your own computer without blocking an entire office, you can use a browser-specific exclusion cookie instead. Swetrix provides a dedicated toggle in the dashboard that places a secure cookie in your current browser, which the tracking script checks before logging a session. This method works well for individual content creators, while IP filtering works better for agencies managing traffic from a static corporate headquarters.

Because Swetrix operates as a cookieless Google Analytics alternative, it handles public IP data with strict privacy controls. According to the [published data policy](https://swetrix.com/data-policy), request IP and User-Agent information are processed transiently in memory. The system hashes these details to generate an anonymized session identifier rather than storing the raw IP address or User-Agent string on disk. This transient processing approach allows you to filter your own public IP dynamically without archiving the personal data of website visitors, keeping tracking compliant with privacy regulations.

## Why an IP Lookup Can Show a Different Result

Checking your public address on Monday and again on Friday might reveal completely different numbers, or an estimated city that places you hundreds of miles away from your actual desk. These discrepancies rarely indicate a technical failure; they reflect how modern network infrastructure routes internet traffic.

When you connect to a Virtual Private Network, your software encrypts your traffic and sends it to a server in another location. That server decrypts the request and forwards it to the target website, so the lookup tool only sees the public address of the VPN exit node. Selecting a server in Frankfurt makes the lookup tool display a German IP address, even if you are sitting in a Chicago apartment. Corporate proxies and privacy relays operate on similar principles, replacing your original network identifier with their own infrastructure address.

Internet Service Providers also influence how addresses appear through Carrier-Grade NAT. Because IPv4 addresses are exhausted, cellular carriers and residential providers often place hundreds of customers behind a single massive router, assigning a private address to your home modem and translating it to a shared public address before the traffic hits the wider internet. Changing cell towers or restarting a residential gateway frequently drops you onto a different shared public address.

MaxMind's GeoIP data estimates locations for IP addresses, but accuracy varies by country, distance, IP type, and ISP practices. Its [geolocation guidance](https://support.maxmind.com/knowledge-base/articles/maxmind-geolocation-accuracy) says the data isn't precise enough to locate a specific household, individual, or street address.

While an IP lookup might accurately identify a specific regional ISP, it cannot identify the street, the building, or the person sitting at the keyboard. If your provider reroutes traffic through a neighboring state for load balancing, your estimated location shifts to match that routing center, making location estimates broad network diagnostics rather than precise tracking metrics.
